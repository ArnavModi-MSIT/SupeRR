How pressure is defined

There is no standard definition, so the model builds one from the match itself instead of using a fixed rule such as "death overs".

1Estimate the win probability

For every legal delivery, a model gives the batting side's chance of winning from runs required, balls left, wickets in hand and the league. It is well calibrated: predicted and observed win rates agree to about one percentage point.

2Measure how much one ball can move it

Take every possible outcome of the next ball, weight by how often each happens in similar situations, and average the swing in win probability. Divide by the all-ball average to get the leverage index.

3Call the top 20% of each innings pressure

Pressure balls are mostly close contests, and they sit disproportionately in the powerplay and the death overs.

How far to trust the ranking

Pressure samples are small. Players have 100 to 450 pressure balls each, so luck is a large part of any raw number.

Do the top ten survive a different modelling choice?

Counts of our ten that stay in the top ten. "Too few balls" means the player fell below the minimum sample in that cut, so the test could not be run.

Where the model is less certain

  • Vaibhav Sooryavanshi, Ashutosh Sharma, Harry Brook and Krunal Pandya rest on only 100 to 135 pressure balls over 15 to 22 matches. A 15-match rule removed Sufiyan Muqeem (11 matches), a third of whose pressure balls came against Zimbabwe, Oman and A-teams.
  • Rinku Singh ranks first on IPL data but much lower on T20I-only and since-2024 samples, so his lead is concentrated in some seasons.
  • Dasun Shanaka's rating is driven by T20I matches; he has too few IPL pressure balls to be ranked on IPL data alone.
  • The model has no venue, pitch, dew or team-strength inputs, and byes and leg-byes are estimated from run margins.
  • There is no price, nationality or availability information, so the ranking shows who performs, not who is a bargain.
